Curriculum Vitae

I am a PhD candidate in Economics at the Bordeaux School of Economics (University of Bordeaux) under the supervision of Eric Berr and Sylvie Ferrari, expected to graduate in 2027. My PhD focuses on the social acceptability of environmental policies in France.

I combine an interdisciplinary framework with quasi-experimental methods to understand how distributive effects, trust in institutions and social movements shape the social acceptability of environmental policies, and the policy change conditions to raise social support for environmental policies.

Work in Progress

  • The social acceptability of environmental policies: definition, quantification, and implications (solo-authored).
    Abstract

    This paper proposes a unified framework for the concept of social acceptability of environmental policies. We first document how public resistance has driven the failure of past environmental policies, highlighting the complexity of the underlying social dynamics. We then review the existing concepts developed to capture this complexity and identify their limitations. Building on the notion of “social acceptability”, we propose a definition of the social acceptability of environmental policies together with a quantification strategy suitable for economic modelling.

  • The green generation? The legacy of Fridays For Future protests among French youth, with Annalí Casanueva Artís (ifo Institute) and Christophe Lévêque (Bordeaux School of Economics).
    Abstract

    This paper examines the causal impact of a youth-led social movement on climate-related attitudes. We study the climate justice movement that took place in France between 2019 and 2021, widely known as “Fridays For Future”, which exposed young individuals to specific environmentalist narratives. Using an event-study design and repeated cross-sectional survey data, we find a two-year increase in environmental concern, a lasting increase in support for state-led solutions to climate change, and higher support for green parties across the generation targeted by the movement. We find a positive effect of exposure to protests at the municipality level, and heterogeneous effects depending on individuals’ socio-demographic characteristics.

  • What is taught in French undergraduate economics programs? Evidence from a survey of bachelor’s degree tracks, with Arthur Jatteau (Université Paris Nanterre, IDHES) and Maxime Galon (Bordeaux School of Economics).
    Abstract

    This paper examines the content of economics teaching in French universities. We assemble a novel database covering 146 bachelor’s degree tracks across 53 French public universities. Unlike previous studies conducted at the university level, our analysis operates at the level of individual degree tracks. We document a strong predominance of standard economic analysis, quantitative methods and management courses, alongside a marginal share of reflexive approaches, limited disciplinary openness, and a near-total absence of qualitative methods. We further construct a multidimensional pluralism index and reveal substantial heterogeneity across track types and within universities.

  • Driven to Protest: Transport Energy Vulnerability and the Yellow Vest Movement, with Emile Baudoin (École normale supérieure – PSL).
    Abstract

    This paper examines the role of transport energy vulnerability in the rejection of the French carbon tax and the Yellow Vests movement. We hypothesize that transport energy vulnerability, which combines income vulnerability with car dependence, is more relevant than transport fuel poverty for understanding the political backlash against the increase in the carbon tax. We conduct two analyses, at the individual level and at the urban-area level, and construct an indicator of transport energy vulnerability for each. We show that vulnerability is associated with a higher probability of rejecting the carbon tax and with more intense local Yellow Vests mobilizations, beyond the effect of income alone. Using a range of specifications, we disentangle the effect of transport energy vulnerability from other known determinants, and we highlight the spatial dynamics of the mobilization.
